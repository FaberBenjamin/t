11 September 2001 and a new security environment

Hijacked planes struck the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, and a fourth crashed in Pennsylvania. The targets were chosen to hit the symbolic heart of American economic, military and political power.

Oct 2002

Bali nightclub bombing: over 200 dead, many of them young tourists.

Mar 2004

Madrid rush-hour train bombs kill around 200 commuters.

Jul 2005

Coordinated blasts on three Underground trains and a bus kill 52 in London.

Oct 2001

First major military step in President Bush’s “war on terror”: the attack on Afghanistan.

Why Afghanistan? Its Taliban government had often backed al-Qaeda, which carried out 9/11 and trained many members there.

Giddens’s point: after 9/11 a different form of terrorism, potentially deadlier than before, became possible.

Terrorism (2 of 5)

What is terrorism?

  • Old practice, new word

    “Terror” dates from the French Revolution of 1789 and was coined by its opponents. Terrorizing civilians is much older, as when conquerors razed whole cities.

  • Shifting moral judgements

    “One person’s terrorist is another’s freedom fighter.” Nelson Mandela was once reviled as a terrorist and is now widely revered. A useful concept must be freed from such shifting valuations.

  • Can states be terrorists?

    States have killed far more civilians (Dresden, 1945, is a debated case). Giddens still restricts the term to groups outside the state, otherwise it blurs into war.

Working definition (Panyarachun et al. 2004)

Action by a non-state organization meant to kill or seriously harm civilians or non-combatants.

Its purpose, by nature or context, is to intimidate a population or to compel a government or international organization to act or refrain from acting.

In short: attacks on civilians designed to change a government’s policy or damage its standing in the world.

Giddens, Sociology (6th ed.), ch. 23: Nations, War and Terrorism2 / 11

Social scientists disagree about whether “terrorism” can be defined objectively. Giddens accepts the difficulty but argues a neutral definition is possible if state violence is treated separately.

The three differences are scope, organizational structure and means. Old terrorism is largely about nations without states; new terrorism is made possible by communications technology driving globalization.

Terrorism (4 of 5)

Al-Qaeda: a “malign kind of NGO”?

  • Origins. “The base”, created in 1989 as Soviet forces left Afghanistan, from Arab volunteers of the 1980s anti-Soviet jihad.
  • Bases. Sudan in the early 1990s, then Afghanistan after 1996 alongside the Taliban. The 2001 US campaign dispersed it.
  • Reach. Estimated cells in around 60 countries and about 20,000 supporters willing to die, mostly semi-autonomous.
  • Idea or organization? Some analysts say the name now covers groups linked mainly by shared aims, ideals and methods.

Like an NGO (Kaldor)

Mission-driven, decentralized, autonomous local cells, supporters in many countries, and quiet links with states (e.g. Libya and Lockerbie).

Like a social movement

Sutton and Vertigans note loose, transnational networks like environmentalism, but al-Qaeda uses symbolic violence rather than non-violent action.

Giddens, Sociology (6th ed.), ch. 23: Nations, War and Terrorism4 / 11

Because authority is spread across cells, the organization stayed strong after its leadership was weakened in 2001. Its members’ moral convictions keep cells functioning.

Terrorism (5 of 5)

War on terror or global governance?

Limits of conventional war

The 2001 Afghanistan attack destroyed some al-Qaeda networks. But new terrorists differ from nation-states in violence, aims and organization, so decisive victory is unlikely. Some question the very idea of a “war on terrorism”, including the 2003 Iraq war.

Hard questions

What proof and international support are needed to act against a perceived threat? Which institutions should deal with a global terrorist threat? What of states that have supported terrorism?

Cosmopolitan response

Kaldor and Beck argue everyone should be treated equally wherever they live. UN, African Union and EU missions hint at this, but mostly act after conflict starts or ends. Prevention is far off.

Think criticallyIf al-Qaeda is at root an ideology rather than a group of people, can a war on terror ever succeed?

Giddens, Sociology (6th ed.), ch. 23: Nations, War and Terrorism5 / 11

Giddens concludes that new terrorism challenges the conventional military approach and raises questions about global governance. Cosmopolitan institutions are a long-term project.

Why can protests escalate?

Possible mechanisms, not proven causes.

In France, student protests over school conditions coincided with broader demonstrations over living costs and public services in October 2026.

Grievances Escalation Interpretation

  • Material insecurity

    Rising living costs, low incomes and housing pressure can intensify social grievances.

  • Institutional frustration

    Poor school conditions and unequal opportunities can weaken young people’s confidence in institutions.

  • Low political efficacy

    When conventional channels seem unresponsive, disruptive protest may appear more effective.

  • Group dynamics

    Crowd interaction, confrontation and widely shared footage can accelerate escalation.

  • Contested framing

    Officials blamed radical actors; some protesters and observers questioned police tactics. These remain competing accounts.

From protest to terrorism: the legal threshold

The decisive question is not only whether violence occurred, but what the act was intended to achieve.

Protest

Riot or violent disorder

Violence linked to a political conflict or aim

Legal thresholdonly with terrorist purpose

Terrorist act

Serious criminal act plus a specific aim to intimidate a population, coerce a government or destabilise fundamental structures.

A separate question: terrorist organisations

Designation is a separate legal status assigned to an entity under a particular jurisdiction. A listed organisation and a protest movement are not interchangeable categories.

EU legal test: serious intimidation of a population, undue coercion of a government, or serious destabilisation of a country’s fundamental structures.

Sociology, Europe 20268 / 11

A protest may remain peaceful, or violence may occur at its edges. A riot can be a serious offence without satisfying the legal test for terrorism.

Under the EU framework, terrorist purpose includes seriously intimidating a population, unduly compelling a government, or seriously destabilising fundamental structures.

National law and the legal status of named organisations vary by jurisdiction.

Movements, labels and possible exploitation

Antifa

A broad, decentralised anti-fascist milieu. It has no single leadership or command hierarchy.

How to assess itLocal groups and tactics vary. Specific acts or violent networks must be assessed individually.

“Free Palestine”

A slogan and broad solidarity movement, not one organisation. Participants and organisers have diverse aims.

How to assess itPeaceful advocacy for Palestinian rights does not, by itself, show support for Hamas or another armed group.

Potential exploitation is not proof of control

Extremist actors may use a cause, slogan or public grievance to recruit, spread propaganda or deepen polarisation. Europol reports that the Gaza conflict was exploited by jihadist, right-wing and left-wing extremists to reinforce their narratives.

A shared slogan, event or online post alone does not prove membership, coordination or support for terrorism.

Sociology, Europe 20269 / 11

Antifa is a broad, decentralised anti-fascist movement rather than one centrally commanded organisation. Individual local groups can have different practices, including confrontational or violent actions, which should be attributed specifically.

“Free Palestine” is a slogan used by a wide range of people and organisations. The EU lists Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad as terrorist organisations, but that does not make the wider solidarity movement a terrorist organisation.

Europol’s 2026 report says the Gaza conflict was exploited by violent extremists across ideological currents, including jihadist, right-wing and left-wing actors, to mobilise support and reinforce narratives. This establishes a risk of exploitation, not that the broader movements knowingly or unknowingly have organisational ties.

Conclusion

How should we interpret the violence?

Grievances

Economic insecurity and institutional dissatisfaction can help explain mobilisation.

Escalation

Crowd dynamics, fringe actors and policing may shape how events unfold. Their roles must be established case by case.

Legal test

Terrorism requires a serious offence and a specific terrorist purpose. Organisational designation is a separate legal question.

Main takeawayA violent incident does not make every protester or an entire movement terrorist.
